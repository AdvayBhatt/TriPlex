The Maize Prediction Handbook

A complete record of the analysis — what was built, what was found, what failed, and every term explained — written so that someone who has never seen a breeding dataset can read it end to end and argue with it intelligently.

Section 1

The brief, in one paragraph

It is January 2008 at a commercial maize breeding company. Roughly 16,000 new maize lines are about to be planted across the Corn Belt. None of them has ever been field-tested. The budget for field plots has just been cut, so only a fraction can be evaluated. The task is to decide which ones get the ground, using only what is knowable before planting: their DNA, their parentage, and the locations where they are scheduled to go.

Everything in this document follows from one verified fact: of the 7,432 lines in the 2008 Cluster 1 cohort, exactly zero had ever appeared in the eight preceding years. Same in Cluster 2 — zero of 8,536. Every population is new. There is no track record to look up, so prediction must travel through genetics.

Section 2

The biology you need, and nothing more

Five ideas carry the entire project.

Inbred line — often just "line"

A maize plant self-pollinated for many generations until it is genetically uniform and breeds true. It is also weak and low-yielding, because inbreeding exposes harmful recessive genes. This weakness is expected and is not what selection targets.

Heterosis — "hybrid vigour"

Cross two unrelated inbred lines and the offspring, the F1 hybrid, is vigorous, uniform and massively outyields either parent. Every bag of commercial maize seed is F1 hybrid seed. So the product is a hybrid, but the thing a breeder develops is inbred lines.

Heterotic pool — in this dataset, "Cluster 1" and "Cluster 2"

Two deliberately separated families of germplasm. Crossing within a pool gives weak heterosis; crossing across pools gives strong heterosis. The programme improves each pool separately and combines them at the end. Hence: C1 lines are only ever crossed to C1 lines, and C2 to C2.

Tester and testcross

An inbred line cannot be judged by its own performance, since inbreds are all weak. So each candidate is crossed to a fixed, known partner from the opposite pool — the tester — and the resulting hybrid is what gets planted and measured. Every yield number in the dataset is a hybrid's yield, not the line's.

GCA and SCA — General and Specific Combining Ability

GCA is how much a line raises hybrid yield on average, across many partners. It is driven by additive effects: each good gene variant contributes a little and the contributions sum. Predictable, and the quantity estimated here. SCA is the extra effect of one specific pairing — two lines that happen to click. Driven by gene interactions, and famously hard to predict.

Why crossing comes after ranking

A natural question: if the product is a C1×C2 hybrid, why not test hybrids directly? Because 500 × 500 is 250,000 combinations, each needing multi-location trials. Instead each candidate is crossed to one tester, which estimates its GCA cheaply. Rank on GCA, keep the best ~20 from each pool, and only then test those 400 specific pairings for SCA. This dataset is stage one only.

SNP — Single Nucleotide Polymorphism, said "snip"

A single position in the DNA where individuals differ by one letter — one plant has a C, another a T. Cheap to measure at scale. There are 2,911 of them, the same panel for every line, coded 1 / -1 for the two homozygous states and 0 for heterozygous (one copy of each).

QTL — Quantitative Trait Locus

A stretch of genome that genuinely influences a measurable trait. Yield is polygenic: thousands of QTL each contributing a tiny amount. The markers here are almost certainly not QTL themselves.

LD — Linkage Disequilibrium

The reason 2,911 markers can predict anything. During reproduction, chromosomes swap segments — recombination. Two positions close together are rarely separated, so they are inherited as a unit. A marker sitting near a QTL therefore travels with it and acts as a proxy for a gene that cannot be measured directly. In biparental families — only two ancestors, few generations — LD is very high, so each marker tags a large chromosome block. That is why a sparse panel suffices.

Six things the documentation got wrong

Each verified against the raw files. The years are 2000–2008, not 2001–2007 — and the 2008 yields are present, which is what makes honest grading possible. The year column is YEAR_x, not YEAR, so the supplied sample code does not run. Every Cluster 2 identifier carries a fourth component (C2.1.1.0), so a naive join matches nothing. There are 405 locations, not the ~7 listed. The genetic map exists only inside the "unimputed" archives. And six populations have malformed progeny identifiers that the guide's own regular expression silently discards.

A detail that shapes everything: the genotypes are mostly inferred

Splitting missingness by row type is revealing. The parents of each population were genotyped densely — 0.8% missing. The progeny were genotyped at only about 3% of markers — 96.9% missing — and everything else was filled in computationally from the genetic map plus the parents' known chromosomes.

This works precisely because of the high LD described above: since a progeny chromosome is a mosaic of a few large parental blocks, ~90 scattered markers are enough to infer which parent each block came from, and from there all 2,911 follow. It also means progeny marker values are ~97% statistical inference, which belongs in any honest limitations section.

Section 4

The statistics toolkit

Every concept used later, defined once here.

Correlation (r)

"When one number goes up, does the other?" Runs from −1 to +1. Zero means no relationship. It is the primary metric here because the decision is a ranking problem.

Pearson vs Spearman correlation

Pearson uses the actual values and asks whether they move together in a straight line. Spearman discards the values and uses only the ranks (1st, 2nd, 3rd…), asking only whether things are in the same order. Spearman matters here because advancement takes "the top 10%" — the order must be right, not the exact bushels.

R² — "R squared", proportion of variance explained

Literally r². An r of 0.186 becomes 0.035, or 3.5%. This is not reported here, and neither does the genomic selection field, because it is measured against a target that is roughly half random noise and so reads as catastrophic when the result is respectable. See section 10.

Heritability and reliability

The share of observed variation that is genuinely genetic rather than environmental or measurement noise. Reliability is the same idea applied to an average: how much of the variation in the line means is real signal. Measured here at 0.49 — about half of what distinguishes one 2008 line mean from another is noise.

Fixed vs random effects, and BLUP

A fixed effect is estimated independently for each level — useful when there are few levels and you care about each. A random effect assumes the levels are draws from a distribution, and shrinks each estimate toward the overall mean in proportion to how little data supports it. BLUP (Best Linear Unbiased Prediction) is the resulting estimate. Shrinkage is the right instinct when you have thousands of levels with a handful of observations each — exactly the situation here.

Ridge regression and regularisation

Ordinary regression with 2,686 predictors and noisy data will chase coincidences — a marker that looks good in 40 lines by luck gets a huge weight. Ridge adds a penalty on large weights, forcing the model to spread credit thinly and stay modest. The penalty strength is alpha; ours is 30,000, which is very strong and deliberately so. Ridge on centred markers is mathematically equivalent to GBLUP / rrBLUP, the field-standard method.

Overfitting

Learning quirks of the training data rather than real patterns. Looks excellent on data already seen, fails on anything new. Ridge's penalty exists to prevent it.

Cross-validation (CV)

Hide part of the training data, fit on the rest, test on the hidden part, repeat. It estimates real-world performance without touching the true test set.

Confounding

Two things varying together so their effects cannot be told apart. There are three important cases: G×E with plot error (section 12), tester with population (every population uses exactly one tester), and year with weather (section 12).

GEBV — Genomic Estimated Breeding Value

The number the model outputs: a line's predicted genetic merit, computed from DNA alone.

MAF — Minor Allele Frequency

How common the rarer of a marker's two variants is. A marker identical in 99% of lines cannot distinguish between them, so markers below 1% are dropped.

Section 5

The prediction target, and why it is not raw yield

Two problems stand between the raw records and a usable target.

One: fields differ enormously. The environment accounts for 68.8% of all variation in plot yield. One line in the dataset gave 163 bu/acre at one site and 263 at another in the same season. A line grown in good fields looks good regardless of its genetics.

The fix: subtract each field's average yield. What remains is "how much better or worse than everyone else in that same field" — a fair comparison.

Two: a single plot is noisy. And 99.8% of line×field combinations contain exactly one unreplicated plot.

The fix: average each line over the ~7 fields it grew in. Averaging cancels noise.

YLD_ADJ(line) = mean over that line's plots of ( yield − that field's mean yield )

One row per line; spread about ±10.7 bu/acre. This number is the estimate of the line's GCA.

Section 6

Leakage: the trap that would have sunk the submission

The obvious move is to predict yield using the other measurements in the file — moisture, plant height, test weight, lodging. You cannot.

It is January. The seed is not in the ground. Every one of those traits is measured at harvest in October, off the very same plot that produces the yield being predicted. A model using them scores beautifully in testing and is impossible to run on the actual decision, because in January the inputs do not exist.

Target leakage

Accidentally giving the model information that would not be available at the moment the prediction is needed — often information derived from the answer itself.

The pipeline names every excluded trait explicitly in code, so the exclusion is visible rather than implied by omission. The cost of excluding them was checked: no harvest trait correlates with yield above |r| = 0.17. Almost nothing is lost.

Those traits remain useful for two legitimate purposes — predicting them from DNA as separate targets, and building a selection index over predicted moisture and lodging alongside predicted yield. They are simply never inputs for yield.

Section 7

The model

YLD_ADJ ~ Ridge( 2,686 SNP markers ), alpha = 30,000

Each marker gets a small weight; a line's score is the sum of the weights it carries. That is the whole model. Not a neural network, not an ensemble — and sections 13 and 14 explain why that is the correct choice here rather than a fallback.

Section 8

Validation, and why ours looks unusual

Standard random cross-validation would mislead here, and the size of the lie is documented in the literature.

Lines within a population are siblings from one cross — nearly identical genetically. A random split puts brothers on both sides, so the model "predicts" a line by recognising its sibling. It scores beautifully, then collapses on 2008 where every family is new.

Leave-population-out cross-validation

Folds are split by family, so all siblings stay on the same side of the split. Harder, honest, and it mirrors the actual task. The rubric calls this "cross-validation appropriate for breeding program structure."

How big is the lie?

Werner et al. (2020) ran both on the same maize data: random CV gave 0.59–0.77; within-family validation gave 0.00–0.18. A gap of up to 0.59. Most published results reporting large wins use the inflated number. This is why the figures here look small and are trustworthy.

Section 10

Why 0.186 is better than it sounds

The outcomes being graded against are themselves noisy. Each 2008 line's "true" value comes from about five single unreplicated plots. How noisy was measured by splitting each line's plots into two random halves and correlating the two independent averages: they agree at only 0.33, implying the line means are about 49% reliable.

The ceiling

A perfect model — one that knew every line's true genetic value exactly — would still only score r ≈ 0.70 against this target. The rest is plot noise, and nothing predicts noise. Any team reporting 0.9 has a leak.

So the honest framing is 0.186 / 0.70 = 27% of what is achievable, not "0.186 out of 1.0". Dividing predictive ability by the square root of reliability gives an estimated prediction accuracy against true breeding value of about 0.27 — though note that this conventional adjustment is known to be statistically ill-behaved, so the measured number leads and the derived one follows with that caveat attached.

Section 11

Where the accuracy actually lives

A line's breeding value splits into two parts that are predictable in very different degrees:

GCA(line) = its family's mean + its deviation within that family

Decomposing the 0.186 to see which part it comes from.

ComponentCorrelationMeaning
Overall, line level+0.186the headline number
Between families+0.316 ranking the 71 new families against each other
Within families+0.109 ranking siblings inside a family
The most important number in this document

95.7% of the variance in the predictions is between-family — but only 34.0% of the variance in the actual outcomes is. The model assigns nearly the same score to every line in a family, while two-thirds of real genetic variation sits within families. The model is effectively ranking families, not lines, and most of the available signal is untouched.

Acting on it: a two-component model

Splitting the problem in two — family mean predicted from the densely genotyped parents, within-family rank predicted from the progeny markers — and blending that with the flat model produces the only improvement in this project that replicated across both clusters.

A tempting result that did not replicate

Classifying families by how related their parents are to already-measured parents gave a clean gradient in Cluster 1 — accuracy of 0.118, 0.126, 0.267 across thirds, more than doubling for the most related. Cluster 2 reversed it entirely: 0.148, 0.180, 0.068. The published literature predicts the C1 pattern, which made it easy to believe. It is recorded here as cluster-specific and is not claimed as a finding.

A sharp illustration: predicting a family's mean from its two parents alone — no progeny markers at all — gives r = 0.196, against 0.277 for the full 2,686-marker model on the same families. Most of what 2,686 markers buy over two parental genotypes is modest.

Section 12

Environment: the biggest lever, and the hardest

Environment is 68.8% of yield variance, so it is tempting to attack it directly. Two findings complicate that, and a third partially rescues it.

G×E is smaller than it looks

G×E — Genotype-by-Environment interaction

When lines reorder between environments — line A beats B in Iowa but loses in Minnesota. If real and large, you need location-specific recommendations. If small, one national list is correct.

Correlation between individual environments is only ~0.10, which looks like enormous G×E. But grouping site-years into six climate/soil types and averaging the noise away tells a different story: cross-type correlation is +0.357 against a within-type split-half floor of +0.265. Performance in one environment type predicts a different type better than two halves of the same type predict each other. The implied genetic correlation across types is high — see the corrected estimate below.

So the near-zero correlation between individual fields was overwhelmingly plot noise, not interaction.

The corrected estimate

An earlier version of this analysis put the genetic correlation at 0.85 by comparing a cross-type correlation against a within-type split-half. That comparison was unsound: the two sides averaged over different numbers of site-years, so they carried different amounts of noise, and taken literally the arithmetic implied a correlation above 1 — which is impossible. Recomputing with both sides measured on matched half-samples:

Two practical notes for anyone reproducing this: fitting environment and population as fixed effects builds a dense 516,000×1,676 design matrix and exhausts R's 16 GB vector limit — random effects use sparse matrices and fit comfortably. And tester must be omitted: all 468 populations use exactly one tester, so it is perfectly nested and carries nothing separable from population.
The formal decision rule

Breeders decide whether to split a programme by regions using CR/DR = r₅ × √(H/Hᵢ) — the gain from selecting on the national mean relative to selecting within a region. Splitting also cuts testing intensity per zone, which counts against it. Even at the conservative end of the corrected range, this comfortably exceeds 1, and above 1 means one national list wins. The break-even sits at r ≈ 0.78; every type pair but one is well clear of it.

Weather predicts places, not seasons — with a twist

Predicting a field's productivity from the 84 weather and soil covariates works across locations (r = +0.185 leaving one location out) and fails across years (−0.030 leaving one year out). Predicting 2008 is a new-year problem.

The working hypothesis was that the covariates are a location fingerprint: half are time-invariant soil, and a location's monthly weather is dominated by its long-run normal. The data contradicted the hypothesis in an instructive way. Splitting every covariate into a location-climatology part and a within-location anomaly part:

The pattern is the finding

Genetic effects here are additive, individually tiny and spread across thousands of markers. Methods that hunt for interactions and thresholds find noise instead. The validation-to-holdout drop tells the story cleanly: ridge does not move, the dense network falls 0.229 → 0.172, and the attention model falls 0.188 → 0.096. Flexible models lean on family resemblance, which is exactly what cannot transfer when every target population is new.

A heavily regularised linear model is not a fallback here. It is the correct model class for this biology.

Section 14

What the literature says — including about this dataset itself

Four parallel literature reviews turned up something unexpected: this dataset appears in published work. Two 2014 Crop Science papers describe 969 biparental maize testcross populations from a commercial programme, 2000–2008, two heterotic groups, 2,911 SNP markers, ~156 lines per cross, a ~1,750 cM map. That matches this dataset feature for feature.

Which means there is a published benchmark for this exact scenario.

Published designWhat it isYield accuracy
Phenotypic selectionline's mean in half the environments vs the other half0.24
Within-familytrain on the same biparental cross0.14
"Same background"pool unrelated crosses → predict a new one — the task here0.06
TriPlexresult on the same task0.186

That is roughly three times the published number for this scenario, and it also exceeds their within-family figure while solving a harder problem. The most useful framing: their phenotypic-selection repeatability was 0.24, so prediction from DNA alone, on unphenotyped lines in unseen families in a future year, reaches 78% of what actually growing the plants twice achieves.

Two questions the literature settles

Would more markers help? No. Marker thinning in this exact germplasm gave 0.45 with 36 markers, 0.49 with 72, 0.50 with 107 — not significantly different. Marker count explained 0.0% of variance in accuracy.

Would more training data help? No. Accuracy here is not size-limited; plugging these numbers into the standard theory implies an effective locus count orders of magnitude beyond anything plausible. Another 60,000 unrelated lines would do almost nothing.

The binding constraint is which families, not how many lines — a relatedness-matched training set has been shown to beat a general one several thousand percent larger.

Why neural networks fail here, mechanically

Ubbens et al. (2021) trained a network shown only where two genotypes match, with no marker values at all. It predicted as well as one with full access. Their conclusion: neural networks in genomic prediction learn overall relatedness, not marker effects. That is precisely the failure signature observed here — relatedness is abundant inside training families and absent by construction in new ones.

This negative result is more informative than most published ones: the ratio of markers to training lines here is two orders of magnitude more favourable to neural networks than almost any published crop dataset. The "needs more data" explanation is therefore unavailable.

Section 15

Limitations, stated plainly

Failure modes

  • Novel germplasm. Accuracy depends on relatedness to trained lines. A 2008 family unrelated to anything in 2000–2007 will predict poorly, and the relatedness analysis in section 11 does not reliably flag which ones in advance.
  • New seasons. Environmental covariates cannot forecast an unseen year. Absolute yield forecasts for 2008 are unreliable; only the relative ranking is.
  • New locations. 37 of 149 sites have no history, so their field effect is assumed zero with the widest interval.
  • Thin lines. A line grown in 2 fields is ranked alongside one grown in 7. Reliability weighting was tested and hurt, so lines are weighted equally — a known compromise.

Data limits that no model can overcome

  • SCA is inestimable by design. Every line is crossed to exactly one tester (0 of 73,532 have two) and every population uses exactly one tester (468 of 468). Line×tester interaction has no degrees of freedom, and the tester effect is perfectly confounded with population. This was verified rather than assumed.
  • G×E cannot be separated from plot error at the individual-field level: 99.8% of cells hold one unreplicated plot.
  • Imputed genotypes are ~97% inferred. Imputation error is a real and unquantified uncertainty source.
  • Genotype coverage is 85% for C1 training years, so ~15% of training lines are unusable. The 2008 prediction set is 100% covered.

The weakest claim in the analysis

The genetic correlation of 0.85 across environment types rests on a reliability correction comparing a cross-type correlation against a within-type split-half. Those two are not computed over matched numbers of site-years, and if they were reliability-matched the arithmetic would imply a correlation above 1 — which is impossible. The direction of the finding is solid and the decision it supports is robust to a wide range of values, but the 0.85 figure itself should not go into the submission until it is re-derived properly, ideally from a factor-analytic model that handles reliability and unbalanced data correctly.

Handover note

On the R script — for whoever picks up the mixed-model work

The lme4 variance-components script was run as requested. It does not execute as written, and separately from the syntax there are two data issues worth knowing about, because the same traps will catch any rewrite. Corrected versions of both scripts are in R/ in the repository.

Three things that stop it running

  1. Unbalanced parenthesis. lmer(yield ~ environment + tester + genotype), data = ...) has an extra closing bracket after genotype.
  2. No random-effects term. That same call has no (1 | ...) term, so lmer stops with "No random effects terms specified in formula". A model with only fixed effects is lm().
  3. An object that never exists. The fitted model is assigned to c1_fixedG_gxe, but anova() and update() are both called on c1_gxe. Also, anova() comparing a REML = FALSE fit to a REML = TRUE one is not a valid likelihood-ratio test — both must be on the same scale.

Two data issues that matter more than the syntax

shorthand_x is the population, not the genotype

It takes 499 distinct values; there are 78,045 distinct lines. Passing it as genotype fits population effects. That is a perfectly reasonable model — and as it happens the only one of the two that is estimable, see below — but it answers a different question from the one the variable name implies. The line identifier is LINE_UNIQUE_ID.

tester is perfectly nested in population

All 468 populations use exactly one tester each. So tester carries no information that population does not already contain; fitting both as fixed effects is rank-deficient and the software will silently drop columns. Tester has to come out of the model.

It also runs out of memory

With environment (1,177 levels) and population (499) as fixed effects, the design matrix is roughly 516,000 × 1,676 doubles and R stops with "vector memory limit of 16.0 Gb reached". Making the terms random fixes this — random effects use sparse matrices — and is also the statistically correct choice, because VarCorr() reports variance components, and a term must be random to have one at all.

The working formulation, which fits comfortably:

lmer(yield ~ 1 + (1 | environment) + (1 | population)
     + (1 | population:environment), data = d, REML = TRUE)

And the instinct behind the interaction term was right, even though the variable was mislabelled. Genotype×environment interaction cannot be estimated at the line level — one unreplicated plot per line per field means it is perfectly confounded with plot error — but at the population level each cell holds a median of 169 plots, so it is estimable. The fit gives environment 62.0%, population×environment 10.9%, population 5.6%, residual 21.5%, which agrees closely with the independent Python decomposition in section 5 and adds an interaction term that analysis could not isolate.

One small thing to watch: read_csv renames the unnamed first column to ...1 and emits a warning. Harmless, but it will appear in the output.

DHdoubled haploid — a way of producing inbred lines in one generation instead of six
F1 / F2 / BCfirst-generation hybrid / its self-pollinated offspring / backcross to a parent
GBLUP / rrBLUPgenomic BLUP — mathematically equivalent to ridge regression on centred markers
GCAGeneral Combining Ability — a line's average contribution to hybrid yield. The prediction target
GEBVGenomic Estimated Breeding Value — the model's output for one line
Germplasmcollective noun for breeding material — the pool of lines and their genetics
G×EGenotype-by-Environment interaction — lines reordering between environments
Heritabilityshare of observed variation that is genuinely genetic
Heterosishybrid vigour — an F1 hybrid outperforming both inbred parents
Heterotic poola separated germplasm family that combines well with another. Here: Cluster 1 and Cluster 2
IBD / IBSidentity by descent (shared from a known common ancestor) / by state (looks the same, origin unknown)
Imputationfilling in unmeasured genotypes computationally, here from the map plus parental chromosomes
Inbred linea genetically uniform, true-breeding plant produced by repeated self-pollination
LDLinkage Disequilibrium — nearby genome positions being inherited together, which is why markers work as proxies
Leakagegiving the model information unavailable at real decision time
MAFMinor Allele Frequency — how common a marker's rarer variant is
MLPmulti-layer perceptron — a standard dense neural network
Pearson rcorrelation using actual values; the primary metric here
Polygenicinfluenced by thousands of genes, each contributing a tiny amount. Yield is strongly polygenic
Populationhere: one cross and all the candidate lines derived from it. There are 999
QTLQuantitative Trait Locus — a genome region genuinely affecting a trait
R²proportion of variance explained; misleading here, so not reported
Recombinationchromosomes swapping segments during reproduction — what breaks marker–gene associations
Regularisationpenalising model complexity to stop it fitting noise
Reliabilityshare of variation in an average that is real signal. The 2008 line means here: 0.49
RKHSReproducing Kernel Hilbert Space — a kernel regression family; competitive in some settings, untested here
SCASpecific Combining Ability — the extra effect of one particular pairing. Out of scope and inestimable here
Shrinkagepulling estimates toward the mean when data is thin — the core idea behind ridge and BLUP
SNPSingle Nucleotide Polymorphism — a single-letter DNA difference. 2,911 are used here
Spearman rhocorrelation using ranks only; matches "advance the top N%"
Testcrosscrossing a candidate to a fixed tester to measure its breeding value
Testerthe known, fixed partner line from the opposite heterotic pool
